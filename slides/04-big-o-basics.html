Track B · DSA · Day 1

How badly does it get worse?

Big-O, with no maths — one room, ten students, and one question you ask every time.

Start at the floor

What "DSA" even means

  • Algorithm = a method for getting something done. That's the whole word.
  • You already wrote one: take water, boil it, add tea powder, pour to cup. That is an algorithm.
  • Data structure = how you arrange your stuff. A pile, a shelf, or labelled jars.
  • For any job there is more than one method — so: which one is better?
1️⃣ The problem

"It took 2 seconds" is worthless

  • Faster laptop → different number. 40 Chrome tabs → different number.
  • Different language, different day → different number.
  • The expensive version: your code works on the 50 rows you tested. Six months later there are 200,000 and the app freezes. Nobody wrote a bug — it grew badly.
  • So the real question: when the data gets bigger, how much worse does this get?
2️⃣ The picture

500 students. Is Priya here?

METHOD A — read every name, top to bottom
   worst case: Priya is last  →  500 looks

METHOD B — the register is alphabetical
   open the middle: "Manoj". Priya comes after M,
   so she CANNOT be in the first half. Bin it.
   500 → 250 → 125 → 62 → 31 → 15 → 7 → 3 → 1
                                    →  9 looks
⭐ The whole idea

Now make it a million

              500 names        1,000,000 names
Method A         500 looks       1,000,000 looks
Method B           9 looks              20 looks

The register grew 2,000× bigger.
Method A got 2,000× worse.
Method B got worse by eleven looks.

Some methods fall apart when the job gets bigger. Some barely notice. That difference is the subject.

⚠️ The framing mistake

Big-O is a price tag, not a menu

You don't choose O(n).

You choose a method — and O(n) is the label that describes it.

Like a price: you pick the item, and the price is whatever it is.

The question is: "what did I do, and what does that cost?"

🔑 The one test

Double the crowd. What happens?

work stays the same    →  O(1)        "doesn't care"
work goes up by 1     →  O(log n)    "barely notices"
work DOUBLES          →  O(n)        "grows with the crowd"
a bit over double     →  O(n log n)  "plus the sorting"
work goes up 4×       →  O(n²)       "falls apart"

Don't memorise five formulas. Ask one question and read off the answer.

1️⃣ O(1) · doesn't care

Ask the student nearest the door

10 students → 1 question
20 students → 1 question

You get this when: you go straight to one thing without looking around.

The room could hold ten thousand. Still one question.

2️⃣ O(log n) · barely notices

Guess my number — higher or lower?

range 10 → ~4 guesses
range 20 → ~5 guesses

You get this when: every step throws away HALF of what's left.

⚠️ Only works because the numbers are in order. Scrambled, "higher or lower" tells you nothing. No order, no O(log n).

3️⃣ O(n) · grows with the crowd

Take attendance

10 students → 10 calls
20 students → 20 calls

You get this when: you must look at every item, once.

📌 If the job genuinely needs touching everything — count them, find the tallest — then O(n) is the BEST that exists. It's not a failure. It's often the right answer.

4️⃣ O(n log n) · the price of order

Line them up by height

10 students → ~35 comparisons
20 students → ~85 comparisons

You get this when: you sort. Almost every O(n log n) is sorting.

Sorting is what you pay to unlock halving — pay once, and every future search costs O(log n).

5️⃣ O(n²) · falls apart

Everyone greets everyone

10 students → 100 greetings
20 students → 400
40 students → 1,600

You get this when: for every item you go through every item again — a loop inside a loop.

🚩 The one interviewers hunt for — because there's usually a way out, and finding it is the test.

🎓 The trap

Find the tallest student

unsorted room        → O(n)        must see everyone.
                                    CANNOT be beaten.
already lined up     → O(1)        tallest is the last one.
                                    Using sorted data is CHEAP.
sort it, then look   → O(n log n)  ~35 comparisons + 1 look,
                                    to answer what 10 looks would.

O(n log n) is the price of DOING the sorting. Using sorted data is O(1) or O(log n).
Sort only when the data will be searched many times.

🧭 When it does NOT matter

The senior signal

  • Small n → pick the simple thing. With 5 items the clever method saves 2 steps and costs readability. Juniors optimise on reflex.
  • 📱 In a real Android app the slow part is almost never the algorithm. It's the network call, the disk read, or work on the main thread.
  • Someone agonising over a 20-item loop while blocking the UI thread has the wrong worry.
Recap

What we locked in

  • Algorithm = a method. Your tea recipe was one.
  • Clocks are useless for comparing methods. Count steps.
  • One test: double the crowd — what happens to the work?
  • O(n) is often the right answer. O(n²) is the one to interrogate.
  • Next: the way out of O(n²) is a labelled jar — hashmaps.
